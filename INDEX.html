Ethics-Embedded AI Governance: Design Checklist and Vocabulary

Use this checklist to evaluate any AI system in health professions education, whether you are building it, buying it, or already teaching with it, then put it into practice with individual and institutional best practices.

Companion to the plenary at Intelligent Teaching: AI in Health Professions Education, Thomas Jefferson University, October 6 to 7, 2026. Presented by Brian M. Green, Health-Vision.AI.

Five minutes? Start with the portable test.

Ask these three questions of any AI tool or vendor.

  1. Where is intent classified, and what happens when it is wrong?
  2. Can any output reach a learner without passing an ethics or safety check first?
  3. Who can see what, and what is deliberately withheld?

A system that cannot answer all three clearly has ethics applied on top of it, not built into it.

Best practices with the tools you already have

Most educators will not build an application like the one in the session; they will keep using institution-approved tools such as Microsoft Copilot or the AI features in Canvas. Governance as infrastructure still applies: move safeguards out of good intentions and into habits, settings, and systems that run every time, even on a busy day.

Individual best practices

Each practice mirrors a stage of the governed pipeline in the checklist above.

Sort the task before you prompt

Drafting and brainstorming are low risk. Anything patient-facing, graded, or about a real case gets closer review.

Mirrors Intent classification and risk tier

Save your guardrails once

Put uncertainty, disclosure, and no-diagnosis rules into saved instructions, a shared prompt template, or a custom Copilot agent where your institution allows it, so they apply every time instead of only when you remember.

Mirrors Ethics and guardrail layers (K2 and K5)

Keep identifiers out

Use categories, not names, dates, or exact ages. Keep patient information and student records out of any tool not approved for them.

Mirrors Entity extraction and PHI prevention

Run the four-failure check

Before using any AI output, ask: did it invent a fact, inherit a label, overclaim the AI, or script a decision?

Mirrors Post-response safety check

Disclose and keep the trail

Label AI-assisted materials, and save the prompt and version behind anything you teach with.

Mirrors Provenance and audit trail

Close the loop

Report failures to your institution's AI governance group, and teach students the three questions of the portable test.

Deployable governance vocabulary

These terms give faculty, developers, vendors, and accreditors a shared language for asking precise questions about AI systems.

26 terms

Transparency–Containment Paradox™
The need to expose enough process, provenance, and performance evidence to build trust, while restricting sensitive operational details to prevent misuse, boundary violations, and privacy risk. It is resolved by design decisions, not defaults.
Governance as infrastructure
Ethics, safety logic, and accountability embedded in the runtime architecture that controls what a system can do, with whom, and under which conditions; contrasted with governance as policy documents applied after deployment.
Agentic AI
AI systems that plan, coordinate sub-agents, hold state, and run multi-step workflows, rather than only answering single questions.
Model risk
Risk that the model itself produces an inaccurate, biased, or fabricated output.
Orchestration risk
Risk that each component works correctly, but a handoff between components routes a request to the wrong place or with the wrong constraints.
Silent orchestration error
An orchestration failure that produces plausible, well-formed output, so nothing appears broken to the learner or the reviewer.
Intent taxonomy
A structured classification of what learners are trying to do, with each intent assigned a domain, boundary conditions, and a risk tier.
Coaching versus decision boundary
The line between requests about HOW to communicate or reason (in scope) and requests about WHAT to do for a real patient (out of scope).
Entity extraction
Pulling structured information from a learner's message, such as clinical, communication, governance, and interaction entities, while blocking identifiers.
Governance entity
An extracted field that downstream safety layers act on, such as AI limitations, bias risk, privacy scope, or human-in-the-loop requirements. If it is never populated, it cannot be governed.
Risk provenance
A record of who or what judged a risk level, so auditors can confirm the system did not borrow an AI tool's own confidence.
Conversational Knowledge Hierarchy (K1–K6)
A six-layer reasoning stack: Foundational Knowledge, AI Ethics and Governance, Contextual Scenario, Communication Micro-Skills, Guardrails and Safety, and Coaching and Reflection. Each layer must be satisfied before the next.
Structurally embedded ethics
Ethics and guardrail checks positioned so that no output can be generated without passing them; contrasted with superficially applied ethics, such as a disclaimer added afterward.
Guardrail
A runtime constraint that blocks, modifies, or redirects a system behavior, mapped to a specific failure mode.
Decline logic
Defined verbal and system-level refusal behaviors, paired with a redirect to an appropriate learning activity.
Risk tier
A level of consequence, from low to high, that determines which guardrails activate and whether human review is required.
Proportional governance
Oversight intensity scaled to the consequence of failure, so high-risk interactions get more scrutiny and correctable issues stay coaching opportunities.
Governance envelope
The full set of constraints, guardrails, oversight, and transparency rules that apply to one use case at its risk tier.
Human-in-the-loop (HITL) trigger
A defined condition that routes a session to human review, such as confidence below threshold, learner distress, or repeated boundary testing.
Calibrated uncertainty
Language that accurately conveys what a finding can and cannot establish; for example, “low risk” rather than “no risk.”
Role-based transparency
Giving each legitimate audience the evidence it needs, from learners to faculty to accreditors, while withholding operational details from adversarial actors.
Interpretability
How well people can understand a model's internal mechanics.
Explainability
Whether a system can give a human-understandable account of why it produced a specific output.
Observability
Whether authorized parties can monitor a system's behavior in operation, through logs, metrics, guardrail activations, and decision lineage.
Information authority versus decision authority
The difference between permission to receive a patient's health information, such as a HIPAA authorization, and permission to make decisions on the patient's behalf, such as an activated health care power of attorney.
k-anonymity
A reporting rule that suppresses any aggregate group too small to prevent re-identification; a common threshold is at least 5 cases.
